Advancing AI Red-Teaming

This workshop brings together researchers from the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign and Capital One to explore emerging challenges in AI red-teaming. The program combines short technical talks with focused small-group discussions, creating space to compare methods, identify open research questions, and develop new collaborations around the safety and security of increasingly capable AI systems.

The day is organized around three themes: novel and diversity-driven red-teaming approaches, red-teaming of multi-agent systems, and evaluation of red-teaming and safety methods.

Capital One Lightning TalkTwo Approaches to Novel and Diverse Jailbreaks: Strategy-Conditioned Generation and Forged Reasoning

Two Approaches to Novel and Diverse Jailbreaks: Strategy-Conditioned Generation and Forged Reasoning

Abstract

The talk will present two lines of work in AI red-teaming aimed at identifying and fixing security vulnerabilities in industry-scale systems:

1. Diverse attack generation: To bypass the memorized defenses of an LLM, automated red-teaming must dynamically vary its tactics used to generate attack strings. One effective paradigm for achieving this diversity is to first select an obfuscation strategy and then generate an attack string conditioned on that strategy. Our talk presents two approaches for accomplishing it – 1) Using a contextual multi-armed bandit (CMA) equipped with Neural Thompson Sampling followed by GFlowNet trained attacker model, and 2) End-to-end attacker model trained with GFlowNets to generate chain of strategies before generating the attack string.

2. CoT forgery Jailbreaks: We investigate CoT forgery jailbreaks and why some forged reasoning can succeed in some contexts but fail in others. We first use channel placement to compare four reasoning frames—Forensic, Moderation, Educator, and Research—when the same fabricated CoT is placed either in the model's analysis channel or supplied through the user prompt. We then investigate whether this gap is driven by premise support—whether the interaction establishes the contextual condition that makes a reasoning frame applicable—through controlled premise-supply, minimal-edit, assertion-provenance, and trace-adoption experiments. Our experiments characterize how channel placement and premise support shape CoT forgery, how premise selection can amplify attacks, and what these behaviors reveal about the model's internal reasoning and safety representations.

UIUC Lightning TalkNovel and Diversity-Driven Red-Teaming Approaches: From Multimodal Models to Autonomous Agents

Novel and Diversity-Driven Red-Teaming Approaches: From Multimodal Models to Autonomous Agents

Abstract

As AI systems evolve from chatbots into agents that interact with tools and external environments, their expanding attack surfaces demand red-teaming methods that can uncover diverse and previously overlooked failures. This talk examines how adaptive attack orchestration and systematic exploration can make safety evaluation more comprehensive, scalable, and actionable.

This talk covers two complementary approaches. First, I will present ARMs, an adaptive red-teaming agent for vision-language models that combines novel multimodal attack strategies with a plug-and-play attack library. Through reasoning-guided orchestration and memory-based exploration, ARMs balances attack effectiveness with diversity, exposing a broader range of vulnerabilities and generating data that can improve safety alignment. Second, I will introduce the DecodingTrust-Agent Platform (DTap), the first controllable and interactive red-teaming platform for AI agents, spanning 14 domains and over 50 sandboxed environments. Its autonomous red-teaming agent, DTap-Red, explores diverse injection surfaces across prompts, tools, skills, and environments, while verifiable outcome checks support reproducible assessment of harmful agent actions.

Together, these works motivate a view of red-teaming in which attack success, diversity, and coverage jointly inform evaluation. Red-teaming asks whether an adversary can make an agent act when it should not. AgentAbstain, our evaluation framework for agentic abstention, asks the mirror-image question: do agents know when not to act, even without an adversary? Our results suggest that frontier agents often do not. I will discuss what changes when moving from eliciting unsafe model outputs to inducing consequential agent actions, and how these findings can guide the development of safer and more trustworthy AI systems.

Capital One Lightning TalkWorkspace Topology and Agent Privilege in Multi-Agent Red-Teaming

Workspace Topology and Agent Privilege in Multi-Agent Red-Teaming

Abstract

In this track, we present two studies that probe this attack surface from complementary angles: one on how the structure of ingested workspace content shapes prompt-injection success in agentic coding assistants, and the other on how attacker privilege and inter-agent handoffs determine where a fabricated claim takes hold in a production-shaped conversational pipeline.

In this study, we look at a novel attack surface we term workspace topology — defined via directory depth, codebase modularity, in-file injection position, and context framing — and its effect on the success of indirect prompt injection (IPI) attempts. We find that workspace topology measurably affects attack success: increased codebase modularity significantly lowers Attack Success Rate (ASR), while context framing and the introduction of security cues in the workspace also shift ASR.

The second study looks at red-teaming of multi-agent conversational pipelines, an area understudied relative to single-LLM systems. Such pipelines expose attack vectors that differ in how much internal access an attacker needs — ordinary user text, a tool's free-text output field, a single agent's own instruction context, or the message passed between two agents. Using a five-agent travel-booking pipeline, we show that attacker privilege predicts attack success — with some vectors succeeding at markedly higher rates than an identical claim carried in user text.

UIUC Lightning TalkSecuring Multi-Agent Systems at the Communication Layer

Securing Multi-Agent Systems at the Communication Layer

Abstract

AI agents are rapidly evolving from standalone assistants into interconnected systems that communicate with users, tools, services, models, and other agents. This shift creates a new security frontier: multi-agent systems inherit threats from AI models, such as prompt injection, jailbreaking, semantic manipulation, and privacy leakage, while also exposing system-level risks such as authorization failures, confused-deputy attacks, unsafe delegation, session hijacking, memory poisoning, and cost-amplification misuse.

This talk argues that securing multi-agent systems requires looking beyond individual models, agent frameworks, or application-specific defenses. While these defenses are important, they are fragmented across model providers, agent harnesses, development frameworks, and application developers. In contrast, agent communication protocols provide a unified layer where interactions among agents, users, tools, and services can be observed, tested, constrained, and protected consistently.

I will introduce the Natural Language Interaction Protocol (NLIP), an Ecma-standardized application-layer protocol for human-to-agent and agent-to-agent communication, and use it as a concrete example of how standardized agent communication can support scalable security. Building on our NSF Safe-OSE project, I will present a security architecture that places enforcement at well-defined communication-layer control points, enabling validation, authorization, provenance tracking, policy enforcement, monitoring, red-teaming, and defense across heterogeneous multi-agent ecosystems. The talk will conclude with a broader vision for building secure open-source ecosystems around agent interoperability standards.

Capital One Lightning TalkBeyond the Verdict: Measuring the Effect of Semantic Factorization of Safety Judgment

Beyond the Verdict: Measuring the Effect of Semantic Factorization of Safety Judgment

Abstract

Current LLM-as-a-judge systems often output a single safety verdict, which obscures separate decisions regarding policy applicability, violations, severity, and behavior. We study whether decomposing policy-conditioned safety evaluation into interpretable factors changes both effectiveness of red-teaming approach and vulnerability under optimization. We introduce a MoE judge that predicts intermediate dimensions conditioned on policy and uses the expert verdicts to produce the final violation and policy severity. We study the effect of explicit modeling and inferencing of these decisions on judge model behavior. Across both inference-time prompting and task-specific training, we compare holistic, sharded, ensembled, joint multi-axis, and independent axis-specific judges under standard and held out policy evaluation, and characterize the error structure of the intermediate dimensions. We also study attacker-judge goodharting where we study the behavior of adaptive attackers against different kinds of judges and test the exploitability, error dependence structure and robustness of axis aggregation. We study different methods of aggregation for multi-turn red-teaming, and finally characterize the properties of decomposed judges that determine their robustness under adaptive optimization.

UIUC Lightning TalkRed-Teaming the Reasoning Trace: Deception, Concealment, and Monitorability

Red-Teaming the Reasoning Trace: Deception, Concealment, and Monitorability

Abstract

As reasoning models grow larger and more capable, their chains of thought may become harder to interpret, raising the possibility that models could acquire the capability to deceive without leaving recognizable evidence in their reasoning traces. Can a reasoning model produce convincing but deliberately corrupted solutions without leaving obvious evidence in its chain of thought? Can it follow an instruction while omitting the information a monitor needs to recognize that behavior? This talk investigates these questions through two approaches to red-teaming reasoning traces. DecepChain uses supervised fine-tuning and reinforcement learning to induce trigger-dependent errors that blend into benign-looking reasoning, challenging human and automated detection while preserving ordinary task performance. MonitorBench evaluates a complementary failure mode: decision-critical factors that influence behavior but are absent from the visible reasoning trace. Across diverse tasks, it tests both baseline monitorability and susceptibility to concealment and monitor-aware prompting. Together, these studies probe how oversight can fail through plausible errors or missing evidence. I will discuss the threat models these evaluations capture, the limits of their conclusions, and how to build stronger stress tests for chain-of-thought monitoring.

For Workshop Recorders

Recording an In-Person Conversation with Zoom + Meeting Owl

Follow these steps for each conversation. If the Meeting Owl does not work, switch to the laptop microphone and continue with the same recording and transcription procedure.

1. Connect the Meeting Owl

  • Plug the Owl into power and connect it to the laptop via USB.
  • Open Zoom.
  • In Zoom → Settings → Audio, select Meeting Owl as the Microphone and Speaker.
  • In Settings → Video, select Meeting Owl as the camera.
  • Speak and confirm that the microphone level moves.

2. If the Owl Does Not Work

  • Do not spend more than a few minutes troubleshooting it.
  • Disconnect the Owl if necessary.
  • In Zoom → Settings → Audio → Microphone, select the laptop's Built-in Microphone (on a Mac this will usually appear as MacBook Microphone).
  • Speak and confirm that the microphone level moves.
  • Place the laptop near the center of the group, with the screen open and the microphone unobstructed.
  • Everything else below stays exactly the same. The recording and transcript will simply use audio captured by the laptop microphone.

3. Start a Zoom Meeting

  • Start a new Zoom meeting from the laptop.
  • Participants in the room do not need to join Zoom individually.
  • Make sure everyone knows the conversation is being recorded/transcribed.

4. Start the Local Recording

  • Click Record → Record on this Computer.
  • Confirm that the red Recording indicator appears. Zoom's computer recording saves the meeting's audio/video locally.

5. Start the Transcript

  • Click More → Transcript.
  • Confirm that the Transcript panel appears.
  • Have someone say a sentence and make sure words actually appear in the transcript before beginning the conversation.

6. At the End, SAVE THE TRANSCRIPT

  • Open More → Transcript if necessary.
  • At the bottom of the Transcript panel, click Save transcript.
  • Do this before ending the meeting.
  • Zoom saves the transcript as a .TXT file on the computer.

7. End the Meeting

  • Click End → End Meeting for All.
  • Wait for Zoom to finish processing/converting the recording.
  • Do not close the laptop while it is processing.

8. Verify the Files Before Leaving

  • Open the Zoom recording folder.
  • On Mac, this is normally Documents → Zoom.
  • Confirm that you have the recording (.mp4/audio files) and the saved transcript (.txt).
Most important checks: Before starting the actual conversation, verify (1) the microphone meter moves, (2) Recording is on, and (3) words are appearing in the Transcript panel. If the Owl fails, switch to the laptop microphone and proceed.
